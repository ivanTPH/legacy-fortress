Your online accounts: leave a map, not your passwords

Email, photos, social media, subscriptions, direct debits, loyalty points. Here's how to make sure your family can find them and deal with them, safely and without breaking any rules.

The short version

  • Write down what you have, not how to get in. The service, the web address, the email or username you use, and what you'd like done with it.
  • Keep passwords in one proper password manager, and say in your Vault where it is.
  • Switch on each provider's own legacy setting. It's the approved way for someone to get your data after you die.
  • Note which phone gets your sign-in codes. Without it, even the right password often won't get anyone in.

Why we never ask for your passwords

Legacy Fortress has no password field, on purpose. Here's why:

  • Safer for you. A place that holds thousands of people's passwords is exactly what criminals look for. If we don't hold them, they can't be stolen from us.
  • They go out of date. Most people change passwords over the years. One written down today is often wrong by the time it's needed.
  • Codes get in the way. Many accounts send a code to your phone when someone signs in, so a password alone rarely works.
  • It can cause problems for your executor. Signing in as someone who has died usually breaks the provider's terms, and can lock or close the account. Providers have their own bereavement process, and executors should use it.

What to record for each account

Add these to your Vault. The account's logo appears automatically for most well-known services.

  • The service and web address, for example Netflix or Gmail
  • The email address or username you sign in with
  • What you'd like done: close it, keep it as a memorial, download the photos first, cancel the payment, or pass it on
  • Where the password is, for example "in my password manager" or "in a sealed letter with my solicitor". Never the password itself.
  • Which phone or app gets your sign-in codes
  • Whether you've switched on the provider's legacy setting, and who you chose

Switch on the legacy settings that already exist

The biggest companies let you choose someone now, while you can. It takes a few minutes each.

Apple

Add a Legacy Contact in your Apple Account settings on an iPhone, iPad or Mac. After your death they can ask Apple for your photos, notes, files and other iCloud data, using an access key and a death certificate. Passwords saved on your iPhone are not included, and nor is anything you bought, such as films or music.

Google (Gmail, Photos, Drive, YouTube)

Set up the Inactive Account Manager in your Google Account. You choose how long Google waits after your last sign-in (between three and eighteen months), who it tells, what they can download, and whether the account is then deleted.

Facebook and Instagram

On Facebook you can choose a legacy contact to look after a memorial page, or ask for the account to be deleted after your death. A legacy contact can't read your messages. Instagram accounts can be memorialised or removed at a family member's request.

Microsoft, X and most other services

Most have no setting to choose someone in advance. Your executor contacts them with a death certificate. Recording the account in your Vault means they know it exists and what you wanted.

Use a password manager, and plan for it

A password manager keeps every password in one encrypted vault that only you can open. It's far safer than a notebook, a spreadsheet or the same password everywhere.

  • Bitwarden, Dashlane and LastPass offer emergency access on their paid plans. You name a trusted person; if they ask for access and you don't refuse within a waiting period you set, they're let in.
  • 1Password doesn't offer emergency access. Print its Emergency Kit and keep it somewhere safe, such as sealed with your will.
  • Whichever you use, record in your Vault which one it is and how your family should get into it.

Don't forget money that goes out, and points that add up

  • Direct debits and subscriptions: streaming, broadband, phone, gym, charity donations and insurance often keep charging after a death until someone cancels them.
  • Air miles and loyalty points: Avios, Clubcard, Nectar and hotel points can be worth real money, and some schemes let them pass to family.
  • Crypto wallets: without the recovery phrase, crypto is usually lost for good. Record where the phrase is kept (a safe, a solicitor), never the phrase itself.

Keep it up to date

Look over your accounts once a year, or when you change phone, email address or password manager. Make sure the people you chose still know they've been chosen.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Provider settings change from time to time; check each company's help pages for the latest steps. For your will and your executors, speak to a solicitor. Last checked: November 2026.
